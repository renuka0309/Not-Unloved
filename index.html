Not Unloved

A house where everything was provided

Three girls grew up in an upper middle class family. They were given a good education, good clothes, proper meals and snacks. All three studied at a reputed CBSE school in their town. On birthdays and festivals they wore beautiful dresses, and their father never counted the cost when the school announced a trip.

Yet the girls could not speak openly with their father. They never asked for anything lavish. They never asked questions. They asked only for pens, pencils and charts, and a chocolate that cost five rupees or less was a treat. Only Charu ever tasted a Kinder Joy, and that happened once.

In that house, love came as provision, and not as closeness. No one ever said the girls were unloved, but they did not feel loved by their father either. It was a bond with a strange, ambiguous shape.

Three sisters, three ways of coping

Ananya, the eldest

Ananya was good at studies, sports and every extracurricular activity. She was not used to failure, and at school she was bold and stood up for herself. At home she learned early to stay quiet. Even when she was curious, she did not ask. She never worried her parents and never asked for anything fancy. At twelve, in the seventh grade, she already showed a maturity that did not belong to her age.

Riya, the middle one

At six, Riya was talkative, even arrogant, and she asked questions all the time. Whenever she asked, she was silenced. By the second grade she had understood the state of her home. She made imaginary friends and talked to them. As she grew older she chose silence in the real world, but inside her mind she never stopped talking. She began living in her dreams more than in reality.

This had a cost. When her parents gave her a task she was often lost in her imagination, so she missed the instructions and finished the work halfway. She was scolded for not listening, though there was nothing wrong with her hearing. They called her absent-minded.

Charu, the youngest

Charu was beautiful, cute and loved by everyone. Whenever something new came home, such as tiffin bags, bottles, pencils or books, she chose first. Riya chose next, and Ananya took what was left. This did not mean Charu received piles of toys. It only meant she had the privilege of choosing first, simply because she was the youngest. She was the one who was fine in that house, though she never made close friends at school. She kept people at a distance, and until the twelfth grade no friend of hers ever visited home.

How it began

The father had once planned to have only one child, a boy or a girl, and to live comfortably. But society and relatives kept taunting him for not having a son, and slowly his mind changed. He began trying for a boy.

The mother had been married while still in the ninth grade. She carried six or seven pregnancies, and many of those babies did not survive. Ananya was the first to live, and at first she was cherished as the only child. For the next baby the father asked the doctor to reveal the gender. It was a girl again. Years later the third child, too, was a girl.

All three daughters had grand first birthday celebrations. But as they grew, something quietly broke. Not one of them ever hugged their father. They always sat a little far from him.

Growing up in silence

Even for small mistakes, the father scolded them. When he was home, the girls did not even speak to one another in a normal voice. They played, giggled and fought only when he was away. The expectations on Ananya were enormous. She was good at everything and behaved well, and still a small mistake brought scolding. The father sometimes even doubted his two elder daughters, though they were never in any relationship and never wanted to be, because they knew what kind of strict family they belonged to.

They were never allowed to go out with friends after six in the evening. Honestly, they did not want to. A group outing meant staying out for seven or eight hours, and if they were even a minute late, their father would come to bring them back. So they preferred to stay inside.

Once, the ninth graders organised a farewell for the tenth graders, an event that usually runs until nine at night. The father came and took Riya home at seven, before it had even begun. The next day her chemistry teacher asked why her father had taken her so early. Riya felt uncomfortable. She made up a reason and went back to her bench, because she did not want to say that her father was strict.

When Ananya's P.E. teacher urged her to join the basketball team, her father refused. She could play in the school playground for fun, he said, but no inter-school or inter-state tournaments. She should choose a career with job security, not sports.

Because the girls knew the answer would be no, they never asked for anything extra. Often their mother learned about school events from other parents, not from her own daughters.

Not a cruel family

It would be wrong to call this a cruel household. Their mother loved all three equally and cherished them. Their grandmother never told them to do household chores just because they were girls. She encouraged them to get jobs and to live comfortably. Their father gave them an education at the best school in town and let them travel on school trips.

There was one moment when Riya, in the fifth grade, finally raged at her father. For once she was fearless, and she scolded him for the way he behaved. He listened quietly and tried to laugh it off, but it hurt him deeply. It was the first time he had heard something like that from his daughter. He shared it with Ananya, and she, too, felt hurt. She scolded Riya for being rude to their father.

The arrival of a boy

After many years, a baby boy was born, nearly thirteen years after Charu. The happiness was as high as the sky. The parents had tried for a son for so long, and they performed many rituals for his birth, including a donation of one lakh rupees to a temple in thanks.

The attention that had belonged to Charu now moved to the boy. He received toys, costly chocolates and anything he asked for. He was never afraid to sit close to his father. He wrapped his little arms around his father's neck. He was never forced to do things, and when he left tasks unfinished, nobody scolded him.

A change that came too late

By the time Ananya was in the final year of her engineering degree, the father had changed. He became gentler. He began to speak to his daughters himself. The girls could now talk among themselves in front of him, and he no longer doubted them.

But it was too late. The change was good, and their mother and the girls welcomed it. Still, they never sat close to him or hugged him. When he sat beside them or asked them to sit near him, they sat for two or three minutes and then moved away. They did not hate him. They never had. They were grateful for everything he had provided. They even wished for closeness. But after so many years, they simply did not feel like sitting beside him. It was something they had never been used to.

When she was little, Riya watched movies about rich children who hated their parents despite all the luxury, and she cursed them in her mind. Why would they hate parents who spent so much on them? As she grew older, she began to understand, because their story felt relatable. Still, the three sisters never hated their father. They only got angry when they were scolded, as any child does.

Children crave love when they need it. If it is ignored for years and then suddenly poured out, they will be happy, but they cannot change overnight.

Some parents think, "We love them so much and provided everything, yet they do not sit with us for even two minutes." But you cannot expect children to grow up silent and then adjust to you automatically.

What this story is really about

I would not call this a pure red flag story. I would call it a story of transition.

From never accepting girl children, to accepting them.
From never providing education, to providing the best education.
From never letting girls go out, to letting them out for a few hours.
From always doubting and questioning girls, to doubting them less, and letting them wear jeans and crop tops.

In a patriarchal society, we cannot expect a sudden shift to treating girls the same as boys. But change is happening, generation after generation. We have moved from sati, widowhood restrictions, purdah and illiteracy, to the abolition of cruel practices, the pursuit of education, public participation, employment and the freedom to move about independently.

There is still a long way to go. Girls should be able to move freely even at night, to wear what they like without judgement, and to feel safe at home, at school, in college, at work and in parks. Questions should stop being aimed at rape victims instead of rapists. Girls should no longer hear, "In the end you will only do household chores." These are such basic things, yet in a patriarchal society they are not basic at all. Change will come, though it may take another ten generations.
